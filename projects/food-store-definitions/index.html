Bottom line.

USDA changed the definition in 2026

Since 2011, the USDA has identified and mapped areas with low access to food, first as the Food Desert Locator and, from 2013, as the Food Access Research Atlas. The atlas has always asked the same question: how many people in a census tract live far from a store that sells a full range of groceries? Until this year, the types of stores were supermarkets, supercenters, or large grocery stores. Dollar stores, drug stores, and convenience stores were not part of the analysis.

In July 2026, USDA split the atlas in two:

  • The Large Retailer Access Map (LRAM) is the original atlas under a new name. It still counts only large grocery retailers and still runs on the 2019 store list and 2010 census tracts used in the 2021 edition of FARA.
  • The SNAP-authorized Retailer Access Map (SRAM) is new. It counts every store authorized to accept SNAP except farmers markets and delivery routes, using a 2025 store list and 2020 tracts. That includes dollar stores, chain pharmacies, and convenience stores, roughly 4 times as many stores nationwide (245,414 against 62,020 large grocery retailers in the current SNAP list).

USDA publishes both, and they give very different answers. But, because of the different vintages of data, comparing them directly can’t say how much of the difference comes from which stores count, because they also differ in store-list year, tract boundaries, and how they place people on the map. This analysis holds all of that fixed and changes only the store definition.

How the measure works

Our key measure asks for every person in Pennsylvania, how far is the nearest store that counts? I identify that distance as a straight line census block by census block, then roll the answers up to census tracts, as the USDA indicates.

Step by step:

  1. Start with people, where they live. The 2020 Census counts population for every census block, the smallest unit it publishes (336,985 blocks in Pennsylvania, many no bigger than a city block). Each block is represented by its internal point, a location the Census Bureau places inside the block.
  2. Find the nearest store. For each block, the straight-line distance to the nearest store that counts, measured along the Earth’s surface. The search covers every store in the country, so a block near a state line can find a store across it.
  3. Pick the threshold. USDA uses different distances for urban and rural tracts: 1 mile in urban tracts and 10 miles in rural ones (with ½-mile and 20-mile variants). The urban flag is USDA’s own, so the threshold for every tract matches theirs.
  4. Count the people beyond it. Everyone in a block whose internal point is farther than the threshold counts as beyond it. Summing those blocks gives the number of people beyond the threshold in each tract, and dividing by the tract’s population gives the share.
  5. Apply USDA’s rule. A tract is low access if at least 500 people, or at least a third of its residents, are beyond the threshold. It is low income, low access (the usual “food desert”) if it is also one of USDA’s low-income tracts.
NoteTwo numbers that both get called “people in food deserts”
  • People beyond the threshold: only the people who live farther than the threshold from a store. This is the number in step 4.
  • People in low-access tracts: everyone living in a tract that meets the rule, including neighbors who live next to a store. Headlines like “X million Americans live in food deserts” use this one, because the designation belongs to the tract.

The second is always larger. This page says which one it means each time.

How USDA measures it

LRAM and SRAM also measure distance differently from each other, and from this page.

  • The Large Retailer Access Map (LRAM) is the original atlas. It spreads 2010 Census population evenly across half-kilometer grid squares and measures from each square to the nearest supermarket, supercenter, or large grocery store, using a 2019 store list and 2010 tract boundaries.
  • The SNAP-authorized Retailer Access Map (SRAM), released in July 2026, draws a circle of each threshold distance around every SNAP-authorized store (as of June 2025) and counts the people outside every circle, using LandScan USA’s roughly 90-meter population grid and 2020 tracts.

Both then apply the same low-access rule as step 5. A circle of a mile around every store and the distance to the nearest store are two views of the same thing: someone is outside every circle exactly when their nearest store is more than a mile away. So the three approaches differ mainly in where they put people and which stores they count.

This page uses straight-line distance throughout, because LRAM only measures straight-line distance and the point here is to change one thing at a time. But SRAM’s driving-distance version is a real improvement. Straight lines can only approximate the distance between places and will always underestimate it.. Measured by driving distance, SRAM flags 872 Pennsylvania tracts as low access instead of 491, and 103 as low income and low access instead of 31. Unfortunately this increase is partly hidden by the broader store definition: the two changes push in opposite directions, so the driving-distance count ends up not far from what large grocery stores alone would give, while describing something quite different.

Holding everything but the store list fixed is the point: it lets the ladder and map below isolate the definition, and “Does it reproduce USDA’s numbers?” checks the method against SRAM.

Why the definition matters

Counting a store as food access assumes people can buy affordable, nutritious food there. That holds for a supermarket or grocery store. It often doesn’t for the stores the broader definition adds. Convenience stores charge more per item and carry few of the staples you’d need to cook healthy meals on a budget such as fresh produce, meat, whole grains. Dollar stores are cheaper but usually carry a narrow selection with unfavorable unit costs, and where they spread they can push out the grocery stores that did. A store that accepts SNAP down the street isn’t the same as convenient access to affordable, nutritious food, but the broader definition counts it that way.

The designation also decides where money goes. Grant programs, healthy food financing, and nonprofit planning use these low-access areas to decide where help is needed. Under the broader definition, Pennsylvania’s low-income, low-access tracts drop from 220 to 36, and 536,149 people in low-income tracts stop showing up, even though many of them still live more than a mile from a grocery store. If programs follow the new definition, those are the places that could fall off the map.

Does it reproduce USDA’s numbers?

LRAM still runs on 2010 tract boundaries and a 2019 store list, so it isn’t a fair benchmark. SRAM is: it uses current tracts and stores, and when this method counts the same stores SRAM does, the two should agree. They do. At the standard thresholds, they classify 97.6% of Pennsylvania tracts the same way. That figure partly reflects how few tracts are low access at all, so the stricter measure is kappa, which discounts agreement by chance: 0.90, conventionally “almost perfect.” The two still differ in where they place people (block points here, a fine population grid for USDA), so the numbers aren’t identical, but they’re close enough that differences across store definitions come from the definition, not the method.Most importantly, we don’t see disproportionate errors in combined low-income low-access tracts.

In urban tracts this method counts 989,315 people more than a mile from a SNAP retailer; USDA counts 1,007,419 (98%). Tract by tract, the shares correlate at 0.95, and 87% of urban tracts are within 5 percentage points of USDA’s figure.

NoteHow much does the block approximation matter?

Everyone in a block gets the distance from the block’s internal point. That’s exact for small city blocks and rough for large rural ones. To bound it, a block counts as ambiguous when the threshold falls within its effective radius (the radius of a circle with the block’s land area), so part of it could sit on either side.

8% of Pennsylvanians live in ambiguous blocks under the large-grocery definition (6% with every SNAP retailer). Counting every ambiguous block as entirely inside, then entirely beyond, the threshold puts low-access tracts at 869–1,163 under the large-grocery definition and 356–651 with every SNAP retailer. These are worst cases, since a real block is rarely all on one side. Even at the extremes the ranges don’t overlap, so the gap between the two definitions is larger than anything the approximation could produce.

Straight-line distance understates how far people actually travel, so every count here is conservative; USDA’s driving-distance figures flag considerably more tracts. ## USDA changed the definition in 2026

<!-- DRAFT (Sean): check against the ERS pages linked below; edit into your voice. -->

Since 2011, the USDA has identified and mapped areas with low access to food, first
as the Food Desert Locator and, from 2013, as the Food Access Research Atlas.
The atlas has always asked the same question: how many people in a census
tract live far from a store that sells a full range of groceries? Until this
year, the types of stores were supermarkets, supercenters, or large grocery stores. Dollar
stores, drug stores, and convenience stores were not part of the analysis.

In July 2026, USDA split the atlas in two:

- **The Large Retailer Access Map (LRAM)** is the original atlas under a new
  name. It still counts only large grocery retailers and still runs on the 2019
  store list and 2010 census tracts used in the 2021 edition of FARA.
- **The SNAP-authorized Retailer Access Map (SRAM)** is new. It counts every store
  authorized to accept SNAP except farmers markets and delivery routes, using a
  2025 store list and 2020 tracts. That includes dollar stores, chain pharmacies,
  and convenience stores, roughly `r py$us_ratio` times as many stores
  nationwide (`r py$us_all` against `r py$us_large` large grocery retailers in
  the current SNAP list).

USDA publishes both, and they give very different answers. But, because of the different vintages of data, comparing them
directly can't say how much of the difference comes from which stores count,
because they also differ in store-list year, tract boundaries, and how they
place people on the map. This analysis holds all of that fixed and changes only the
store definition.

Step by step:

1. **Start with people, where they live.** The 2020 Census counts population
   for every census block, the smallest unit it publishes (336,985 blocks in
   Pennsylvania, many no bigger than a city block). Each block is represented by
   its internal point, a location the Census Bureau places inside the block.
2. **Find the nearest store.** For each block, the straight-line distance to the
   nearest store that counts, measured along the Earth's surface. The search
   covers every store in the country, so a block near a state line can find a
   store across it.
3. **Pick the threshold.** USDA uses different distances for urban and rural
   tracts: 1 mile in urban tracts and 10 miles in rural ones (with ½-mile and
   20-mile variants). The urban flag is USDA's own, so the threshold for every
   tract matches theirs.
4. **Count the people beyond it.** Everyone in a block whose internal point is
   farther than the threshold counts as beyond it. Summing those blocks gives
   the number of people beyond the threshold in each tract, and dividing by
   the tract's population gives the share.
5. **Apply USDA's rule.** A tract is *low access* if at least 500 people, or at
   least a third of its residents, are beyond the threshold. It is *low income,
   low access* (the usual "food desert") if it is also one of USDA's
   low-income tracts.

- **People beyond the threshold**: only the people who live farther than the

- **People in low-access tracts**: everyone living in a tract that meets the
  rule, including neighbors who live next to a store. Headlines like "X million
  Americans live in food deserts" use this one, because the designation belongs
  to the tract.

The second is always larger. This page says which one it means each time.

## How USDA measures it

<!-- DRAFT (Sean): check against the ERS documentation linked below. -->

LRAM and SRAM also measure distance differently from each other, and from this
page.

- **The Large Retailer Access Map (LRAM)** is the original atlas. It spreads 2010
  Census population evenly across half-kilometer grid squares and measures from
  each square to the nearest supermarket, supercenter, or large grocery store,
  using a 2019 store list and 2010 tract boundaries.
- **The SNAP-authorized Retailer Access Map (SRAM)**, released in July 2026, draws
  a circle of each threshold distance around every SNAP-authorized store (as of
  June 2025) and counts the people outside every circle, using LandScan USA's
  roughly 90-meter population grid and 2020 tracts.

Both then apply the same low-access rule as step 5. A circle of a mile around
every store and the distance to the nearest store are two views of the same
thing: someone is outside every circle exactly when their nearest store is more
than a mile away. So the three approaches differ mainly in where they put people
and which stores they count.

| | This page | USDA LRAM (original atlas) | USDA SRAM |
|---|---|---|---|
| Where people are | 2020 census block internal points | 2010 Census, spread over 0.5 km grid squares | LandScan USA, ~90 m grid |
| Stores | SNAP Retailer Locator, August 2026; any combination of types | Large grocery retailers, 2019 list | All SNAP retailers except farmers markets, June 2025 |
| Distance | Straight line to the nearest store | From each grid square to the nearest store | Circles around each store; straight-line and driving versions |
| Tracts | 2020 | 2010 | 2020 |
| Can change which stores count? | Yes | No | No |

This page uses straight-line distance throughout, because LRAM only measures straight-line distance and the point here is to change one thing at a time. But SRAM's driving-distance version is a real improvement. Straight lines can only approximate the distance between places and will always underestimate it.. Measured by driving distance, SRAM flags 872 Pennsylvania tracts as low access instead of 491, and 103 as low income and low access instead of 31. Unfortunately this increase is partly hidden by the broader store definition: the two changes push in opposite directions, so the driving-distance count ends up not far from what large grocery stores alone would give, while describing something quite different.

Holding everything but the store list fixed is the point: it lets the ladder
and map below isolate the definition, and "Does it reproduce USDA's numbers?"
checks the method against SRAM.

## Why the definition matters


Counting a store as food access assumes people can buy affordable, nutritious
food there. That holds for a supermarket or grocery store. It often doesn't for
the stores the broader definition adds. Convenience stores charge more per item
and carry few of the staples you'd need to cook healthy meals on a budget such as fresh
produce, meat, whole grains. Dollar stores are cheaper but usually carry a
narrow selection with unfavorable unit costs, and where they spread they can push out the grocery stores
that did. A store that accepts SNAP down the street isn't the same as convenient
access to affordable, nutritious food, but the broader definition counts it that
way.

The designation also decides where money goes. Grant programs, healthy food
financing, and nonprofit planning use these
low-access areas to decide where help is needed. Under the broader definition,
Pennsylvania's low-income, low-access tracts drop from `r py$lila_large` to
`r py$lila_all`, and `r py$lila_pop_drop` people in low-income tracts stop
showing up, even though many of them still live more than a mile from a grocery
store. If programs follow the new definition, those are the places that could
fall off the map.

## Does it reproduce USDA's numbers?

LRAM still runs on 2010 tract boundaries and a 2019 store list, so it isn't a fair benchmark. SRAM is: it uses current tracts and stores, and when this method counts the same stores SRAM does, the two should agree. They do. At the standard thresholds, they classify `r py$agree_la` of Pennsylvania tracts the same way. That figure partly reflects how few tracts are low access at all, so the stricter measure is kappa, which discounts agreement by chance: `r py$kappa_la`, conventionally "almost perfect." The two still differ in where they place people (block points here, a fine population grid for USDA), so the numbers aren't identical, but they're close enough that differences across store definitions come from the definition, not the method.Most importantly, we don't see disproportionate errors in combined low-income low-access tracts.

**Tract designations.** With every SNAP retailer counted, this method and USDA
agree on `r py$agree_la` of Pennsylvania tracts' low-access status (Cohen's kappa
`r py$kappa_la`; LILA kappa `r py$kappa_lila`).

## How much does the block approximation matter?

Everyone in a block gets the distance from the block's internal point. That's
exact for small city blocks and rough for large rural ones. To bound it, a block
counts as *ambiguous* when the threshold falls within its effective radius (the
radius of a circle with the block's land area), so part of it could sit on
either side.

`r py$amb1` of Pennsylvanians live in ambiguous blocks under the large-grocery
definition (`r py$amb5` with every SNAP retailer). Counting every ambiguous block
as entirely inside, then entirely beyond, the threshold puts low-access tracts at
`r py$b1` under the large-grocery definition and `r py$b5` with every SNAP
retailer. These are worst cases, since a real block is rarely all on one
side. Even at the extremes the ranges don't overlap, so the gap between the two
definitions is larger than anything the approximation could produce.

## What this can and can't say

Straight-line distance understates how far people actually travel, so every count here is conservative; USDA's driving-distance figures flag considerably more tracts. Even so, the choice of stores matters more than the choice of distance: with straight-line distance, counting only large grocery stores puts `r py$beyond_ratio` times as many Pennsylvanians beyond the threshold as counting every SNAP retailer.
